# For senior centers, libraries, and assisted living communities

If you run activities, a front desk, or a resident services office, you are
positioned better than any website to prevent this. You see people daily. You
notice when someone's rattled.

Everything here is free. Print it, copy it, translate it, put your own logo on
it, hand it out. You don't need to ask us, just keep a small credit line to
the Trust But Verify Project somewhere reasonable (it's [CC BY-NC](/terms)).
That license is non-commercial, but using it at your facility is explicitly
fine even though you're a business. The license stops someone from selling
this material itself, not from a nursing home or senior center handing it to
residents for free, which is exactly what it's for.

---

## The four things that actually reduce losses

### 1. Put the fridge sheet in every unit

Not a stack at the desk. In the unit, on the refrigerator, where someone standing
in their kitchen holding a phone can see it.

[Print the fridge sheet](/printables), large type, one page, 45 languages.

**Print and distribute the English version freely.** The other 44 languages are
AI translations awaiting a native speaker's review, and they carry a warning band
saying so. Please don't put those in units yet. If you have a bilingual staff
member or resident who'd read one, [that's exactly who we
need](/help-translate), and it would unlock that language for every facility,
not just yours.

### 2. Make the front desk a verification stop

The single most valuable thing a facility can offer is a **person to check with**
who is available right now and doesn't need to be phoned.

Post it plainly:

> **Got a call that scared you? Bring it to the front desk before you do
> anything. That's what we're here for. No question is silly.**

The reason this works: scammers keep people on the phone specifically so they
can't consult anyone. A desk twenty feet away breaks that.

### 3. Train your staff on the four tells

Anyone at your desk should know to gently intervene when a resident:

- Is on a long call and seems distressed or is being told what to say
- Asks for a ride to a store to buy several gift cards
- Asks for help wiring money or is expecting a courier
- Suddenly needs cash and won't say why, or has been told to keep it quiet

One sentence, no accusation: *"That sounds stressful. Would you like me to sit
with you while you call the bank on the number on your card?"*

### 4. Run the talk twice a year

Not once. Twice, and again after any incident in the building. Scam tactics
change, and repetition is what makes the three steps automatic instead of
remembered.

The [5-minute lobby talk](/printables) needs no slides and no expertise. An
activities coordinator can deliver it cold.

## Running a session that works

**Keep it under 30 minutes.** Attention, not content, is the constraint.

**Open with a story, not statistics.** Ideally a local one. "A resident in this
building got a call last month" beats any federal number ever published.

**Never make anyone raise their hand about being scammed.** Not even anonymously.
Not even as an icebreaker. The single most important thing in the room is that
nobody feels exposed.

**Give the three steps early and repeat them at least four times.** People will
remember three things. Make sure they're the right three.

**Leave twelve minutes for questions.** Questions are where the actual education
happens, and someone will describe something that happened to them last week.

**Have handouts in hand as people leave**: not on a table by the door. Hand
them over personally.

**Say plainly: this happens to everyone.** Include yourself. Tell them about a
text that almost got you. It's the permission slip that lets a room talk
honestly.

## What to keep at the front desk

- A stack of wallet cards
- One laminated fridge sheet at the desk itself
- The two helplines on a card by the phone:
  **833-372-8311** (National Elder Fraud Hotline)
  **877-908-3360** (AARP Fraud Watch)
- Your local police non-emergency number
- A note on who to tell internally if a resident reports a loss

## If a resident has lost money

They will be embarrassed, and embarrassment is the reason it goes unreported and
happens again.

1. "I'm glad you told me", first, before anything else.
2. Sit with them while they call their bank on a number from their own card.
3. Offer to sit with them while they call **833-372-8311**. That hotline exists
   for exactly this and will walk them through reporting.
4. Ask whether they'd like family told, and respect the answer.
5. Watch for the [recovery scam](/scams/recovery-scam) in the following weeks. It
   is coming, and it targets people who have already lost money once.

## What you're actually building

A building where people check with each other is a building scammers do poorly
in. Not because anyone became suspicious, because asking got easy.

The residents who learn this will teach each other. That's the part that outlasts
any handout you print: a hallway where "let me ask at the desk first" is just
what people do.

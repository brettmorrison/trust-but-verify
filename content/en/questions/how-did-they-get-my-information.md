# How did scammers get my information?

**Mostly legally, and mostly cheaply.**

That answer matters, because the whole reason a caller recites your address and
the last four of your card is to make you think only your bank could know that.

Anyone can know that. For about a dollar.

---

## The five sources

**1. Data brokers.** Companies whose entire business is compiling and selling
files on individuals: name, current and past addresses, age, phone numbers,
relatives, home value, estimated income, buying habits. Legal in most of the
United States. There are hundreds of them.

**2. Breaches.** Companies you've done business with get hacked, insurers,
retailers, hospitals, credit bureaus, phone companies. Those files circulate for
years. Almost every American adult is in several.

**3. Public records.** Property deeds, voter registration, court filings,
business filings, marriage and death records, obituaries. Obituaries in
particular are a gift to scammers: they list surviving relatives by name and
relationship.

**4. Social media.** Birthdays, pets, schools, grandchildren's names, the town
you grew up in, where you're on vacation right now, which is also, not
coincidentally, the answers to most security questions.

**5. Lists of previous victims.** If you've been scammed once, that fact gets
sold. It's called a sucker list and it's why the [second
scam](/scams/recovery-scam) always comes.

---

## What this means in practice

**A caller knowing your details is not evidence of anything.**

They may know your name, address, birthday, bank's name, last four digits,
children's names, and that you bought a hearing aid in 2019. None of it means
they work where they say they work.

This is the whole reason [the first step](/the-three-steps) is to hang up and
call a number you looked up yourself. It's the only test that works, and it works
regardless of what they know.

---

## Can I get it taken down?

Partly. You won't get it all, and you shouldn't spend your life trying, but a
few hours does reduce your exposure meaningfully.

**Free, and worth doing:**

- **Freeze your credit** at all three bureaus. Biggest single win.
- **Opt out of the big data brokers.** Start with Spokeo, Whitepages, BeenVerified,
  Intelius, Radaris, and PeopleFinders. Each has an opt-out page.
- **Opt out of pre-approved credit offers** at optoutprescreen.com or
  888-567-8688. Cuts down mail theft too.
- **Register at donotcall.gov.** It won't stop criminals, who ignore it, but it
  thins out the legitimate calls so the odd ones stand out.
- **Set social media to friends-only**: yours, and encourage grandchildren to do
  the same. Their public videos are what voice cloning uses.
- **Google your own name** and see what comes up. It's what a scammer does first.

**Paid services** that remove you from brokers repeatedly do work, but the data
gets re-listed, so it's an ongoing subscription rather than a fix. Reasonable
choice, not a necessary one.

## Don't spend your worry here

You can't undo what's already out. And you don't need to, the whole defense on
this site is built to work *even though* they know things about you.

Reducing your exposure is good hygiene. **Hanging up and calling a number you
looked up yourself is the actual protection.**

## Don't spend your worry here

You can't undo what's already out, and you don't need to. Every defense on this
site is built to work perfectly well against someone who knows your name, your
address, and your bank.

Reduce your exposure because it's sensible. Then let it go, and keep the habit
that actually decides the outcome: hang up, look it up, call back.

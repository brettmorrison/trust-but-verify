# The Phantom Hacker

This is the one that takes everything.

Not a few hundred dollars — entire retirement accounts, entire savings, sometimes
a house. The FBI named it and warned about it specifically because of how much
older adults lose to it, and because of how well it's built.

It's built in three acts, usually over several days, with three different callers
who each seem to confirm the last one.

---

## Act 1 — The tech support call

It starts with a popup, or a call about a subscription renewal, or an email about
a charge you don't recognize. You call the number. A helpful technician takes a
look at your computer.

Then he tells you something worse than a virus: **foreign hackers are inside your
system, and they may be inside your bank accounts.**

He says he'll transfer you to your bank's fraud department to check.

## Act 2 — The bank call

A new person calls, or you're transferred. Caller ID shows your bank's real name
and real number. (This is trivially easy to fake — see [can caller ID be
faked](/questions/can-caller-id-be-faked).) They know your bank. They may know
your account's last four digits.

They confirm it: there's unauthorized activity. Your money is in danger. And now
comes the sentence the whole scheme was built to deliver:

> **"For your protection, we need to move your funds to a secure account while we
> investigate."**

Sometimes they add that a bank employee may be involved, so you mustn't discuss
this with anyone at your branch. Sometimes they say the account is under federal
monitoring.

## Act 3 — The government call

If you hesitate, a third voice arrives with more authority: the Federal Reserve,
the FTC, the FBI, the Treasury. They may email you an official-looking letter on
a real-looking letterhead.

They confirm everything the first two said, and instruct you to wire the money —
or convert it to gold, or cash, or cryptocurrency — for safekeeping.

Then a courier comes to your door. Or you drive to a Bitcoin machine while
they stay on the phone with you.

The money is gone the same day.

---

## Why it works on careful people

Because it's designed to defeat exactly the instinct that protects you.

**You verify.** But you verify with a second caller who is part of the same crew.
The independent confirmation you correctly looked for is manufactured.

**It's slow.** It takes days. It doesn't feel like a snap decision, so it doesn't
trip the alarm you have for pressure.

**You're the hero of it.** You're not being asked to hand over money. You're being
asked to *protect* your money from criminals, in cooperation with the good guys.
Being cautious feels like participating.

**It isolates you.** "Don't tell the branch." "Don't discuss an active
investigation." "A bank employee may be compromised." By the time you'd normally
ask someone, you've been told that asking is dangerous.

---

## The four sentences that end it

Learn these. Any one of them is enough on its own.

**1. Your bank will never ask you to move money out of your bank.**
That request, from anybody, in any wording, is a scam. There are no exceptions to
this and there never have been.

**2. There is no such thing as a government safe account.**
Not at the Federal Reserve, not at Treasury, not at the FTC. The Federal Reserve
does not hold accounts for individuals. It never has.

**3. No agency converts your savings to gold or Bitcoin for safekeeping.**
Read that sentence again in a calm room. It sounds like what it is.

**4. Nobody comes to your house to collect valuables.**
No federal agency sends a courier. If someone's coming to your door for money,
gold, or cash, call the police — the real ones, at 911 or your local
non-emergency number.

---

## If you're in the middle of it right now

Stop. Do not send anything today.

Then, on a number **you** look up — off your card, off your statement, or by
walking into a branch:

1. Call your bank and say: *"I need to verify whether anyone from this bank
   contacted me about moving my funds."*
2. Tell them exactly what you were told, including the part about not telling
   them. Fraud departments know this scheme intimately.
3. Call **833-372-8311** (National Elder Fraud Hotline) if you'd like someone to
   talk it through with first.

Waiting one day costs you nothing if it's real. If it isn't real, it saves
everything.

## Why this one loses to you

The Phantom Hacker is the most elaborate scheme in this whole catalog. Three
callers, several days, forged letters, a script built to defeat careful people.

And it dies the instant you walk into a branch or dial the number on your own
card. All that machinery, undone by one phone call you chose to make.

That's worth remembering. The more complicated a scheme is, the more places it
has to break — and you only need to break it once.

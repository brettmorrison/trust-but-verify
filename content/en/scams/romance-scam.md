# The person who was never real

Two things need saying before anything else.

**One: this is not about being lonely and foolish.** It's about being targeted by
someone doing a job, often for months, often as part of a team working from a
script with quotas and supervisors. Some of the people running these are
themselves trafficked and held against their will in compounds. This is organized
industry, not a chance encounter that went wrong.

**Two: the loss is not just money.** A relationship you valued turns out to have
been a performance aimed at your bank account. That is a real bereavement and it
deserves to be treated as one.

---

## How it's built

**Contact.** Facebook, Words with Friends, a dating site, Instagram, a church
group, a widow's support forum, a "wrong number" text that turns friendly. Sites
for older adults, faith groups, and recently widowed people are worked
deliberately.

**Alignment.** They share your faith, your politics, your hobby, your loss.
They're a widower too. It feels uncanny. It is: they read your profile first.

**Intensity.** Fast and total. Good morning texts, good night texts, calls that
last hours, "I've never felt this before," talk of a future within weeks. It's
called love bombing, and speed is a feature, it builds attachment before
skepticism can form.

**A reason they can't meet.** Offshore oil rig. Military deployment. Surgeon with
Doctors Without Borders. Engineer in Dubai. (The rig turns up in two different
scams, as a fake boyfriend's job here, and as a fake job offered to *you* in
[the job that doesn't exist](/scams/job-scams). Same costume, different con.)
Always far away, always with a job
that explains the distance and confers respectability. Meetings get planned and
then something always happens.

**The first ask, which is small.** Never money at first. A phone card. Help with
a small customs fee. Something you can easily do. It's a test, of whether you'll
say yes, and of whether you'll tell anyone.

**Escalation.** A medical emergency. Equipment seized at customs. A frozen bank
account. A contract about to close. Each one urgent, each with a promise of
repayment. They never end, because the story is designed not to.

**Isolation.** Your family "doesn't want you to be happy." Your daughter is
"jealous." Keep it private for now. By the time the asks get large, you have
nobody left who's allowed to have an opinion.

**The investment turn.** Increasingly, this ends in an investment platform rather
than a direct request. See [investment and crypto](/scams/investment-and-crypto).
This version, a relationship built for months and then converted into a fake
trading account, is the single costliest scam pattern in America right now.

---

## The signs

- Never met in person, no matter how long it's been
- Always something that stops a video call, or the video is brief and strange
- Photos too polished, or a small number reused (search them, right-click,
  search image)
- Falls in love very fast
- Job that explains the distance: rig, deployment, overseas contract
- Any money at all, cards, wire, crypto, a package to forward
- Asks you to receive money or forward packages (this makes you a money mule,
  which carries legal risk of its own)
- Discourages you from telling family
- Repayment always promised, always delayed

---

## What to do

**If you're wondering about someone right now:**

1. **Search their photo.** Save it, then use Google Images or TinEye to search by
   image. Stolen photos usually surface under other names in seconds.
2. **Ask for a video call, right now, unplanned.** Have them wave a hand in front
   of their face.
3. **Tell one person you trust.** Say the whole thing out loud. If your first
   feeling is that you don't want to, notice that feeling. It was put there on
   purpose.
4. **Send nothing.** Not a card, not a fee, not a small favor.
5. **Call 877-908-3360** (AARP Fraud Watch). They talk people through exactly
   this every day and they don't lecture.

**If you've already sent money:**

Stop sending. Do not tell them you know, just stop. Then work the list at
[I think I was scammed](/i-think-i-was-scammed).

Expect a barrage: love, then guilt, then anger, then threats, then a "different
person" contacting you to help recover your money. That last one is the
[recovery scam](/scams/recovery-scam) and it's usually the same crew.

**If someone you love is in one:**

Do not lead with "he's fake." You will lose, because you're arguing against
months of daily contact and the scammer has already prepared them for you by
name.

Ask questions instead. *"What does he say when you suggest a video call?"* Let
them find the shape themselves. Stay in their life even while you disagree,
isolation is the mechanism, and the most useful thing you can be is the person
still there when it collapses.

---

## Afterward

Grief here is legitimate. Something ended, even though it was never what you
believed. Anger, embarrassment, and missing them can all be true at once, and
missing them is not a sign you haven't understood.

**AARP runs free, confidential support groups for people who've been through
this.** Call 877-908-3360. Talking to people who've experienced the same thing
helps more than almost anything else, because it removes the part where you think
you're the only one.

## You are not diminished by this

Being open to connection is not a flaw, and the people who exploit it are the
ones who should carry the shame. You wanted to trust someone. That's the better
instinct, and it's worth keeping.

What you can add is one small thing: nobody who has never sat across a table from
you gets money. Everything else about you (the warmth, the willingness, the
generosity) stays exactly as it is.

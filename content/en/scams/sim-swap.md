# My phone suddenly has no signal

This one doesn't start with a call. It starts with your phone going quiet —
no signal, no texts, "SOS only" in the corner — for no reason you can
explain.

---

## What's actually happening

A scammer who already has some of your information — often from a data
breach or a phishing text — calls your cell carrier pretending to be you.
They report your phone "lost" or "damaged" and ask to move your number to a
new SIM card, which they control.

Once that happens, your phone stops working, and every text message meant
for you — including the security codes your bank, email, and other accounts
send to confirm it's really you — goes to them instead.

**What they want:** your phone number, so they can pass your bank's or
email's two-factor security check as if they were you.

**The truth:** carriers are supposed to verify your identity before moving a
number. Scammers get around this by having already collected your name,
address, or the last four of your Social Security number from a data breach
or an earlier phishing message — enough to sound convincing to a call center
employee.

---

## The signs

- **Your phone suddenly shows no service**, with no outage in your area
- **You stop getting calls or texts** that you were expecting
- **You get an email confirming a SIM change or new device**, that you didn't
  request
- **You're locked out of an account** you use often

Any one of these on its own could be nothing. All of them together, or a
lockout right after one, is the pattern.

---

## What to do right away

**Call your cell carrier immediately** — from another phone, since yours may
not work — and ask directly whether your number was ported or your SIM was
changed. Every major carrier has a fraud department that handles this.

**Check your bank and email for unrecognized activity.** If the scammer had
your number for even a short window, they may have already used it to reset
a password.

**Change your passwords**, starting with email and your bank, from a device
you trust.

---

## Before it happens

**Set a PIN or passcode on your cell account.** Every major carrier lets you
add one that's required before anyone — including someone claiming to be you
— can make changes. This is the single most effective step, and it takes
about five minutes on your carrier's website or in a store.

**Use an authenticator app instead of text messages** for accounts that offer
it (your bank, email, and anywhere with real money or sensitive information).
A code generated on the app itself can't be intercepted by moving your phone
number, the way a text code can.

---

A SIM swap is quiet by design — nobody calls to warn you. The carrier PIN is
the one step that stops it before it starts, and it's worth setting today,
not after.

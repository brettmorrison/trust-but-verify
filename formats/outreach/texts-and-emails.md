# Ready to send

Copy, paste, adjust. Written to sound like a person, not a pamphlet.

The rule behind all of these: **never open with a warning.** A warning makes the
other person the subject. Make yourself the subject, or make it a shared rule, or
ask for their help.

---

## To a parent — the one that works best

> Mom, I almost got taken by a text about a package delivery last week. Felt
> stupid about it. Have you been getting those?

Then, when they answer:

> There's a thing I've started doing — if anybody contacts me about money, I hang
> up and call the number on my own card instead of theirs. Sounds simple but
> apparently it's the whole ballgame.

---

## To a parent — asking for help instead of giving it

> Dad, would you look at something for me? I'm helping put together a one-page
> sheet about phone scams for the community center. Is it clear enough? You'd
> tell me if it was condescending, right?

---

## Setting the family rule

> I want us to have a family rule and I'm including myself in it. Nobody in this
> family moves money because of a phone call, text, or email they didn't start.
> We hang up and call back on a number we look up. Even if it's me calling you.
> Especially if it's me calling you.

---

## The code word

> Family thing I want to set up. There's software now that copies somebody's
> voice from a few seconds of video, and people are using it for those "Grandma
> I'm in trouble" calls.
>
> So let's pick a word. Something we'd all know and nobody else would. Then if
> anyone ever calls sounding upset and asking for money, we ask for the word
> first.
>
> Takes two minutes and it beats the whole thing. What should it be?

---

## After a close call — to a parent

> That was a good catch. Seriously. Most people don't catch those.
>
> One thing worth knowing: they usually try again in a few weeks with a different
> story, sometimes pretending to be somebody helping you recover money. Same
> people. If anything like that comes in, send it to me and we'll look at it
> together.

---

## After someone lost money

Send this before anything else. The first message decides whether there's a
second one.

> I'm really glad you told me. This happens to a lot of people and the people
> doing it are professionals — it's not a reflection on you.
>
> Let's just work the list. Can I call you in ten minutes?

**Never send:** "Why didn't you call me first?" — even though you'll want to.

---

## To siblings

> Hey — I've been reading about how much of this stuff is aimed at people Mom and
> Dad's age. Seven billion dollars last year from people over 60, per the FBI.
>
> Three things I want us to set up, and they take about an hour total:
>
> 1. A family code word for emergency calls
> 2. Credit freezes for them — free, and reversible
> 3. A "trusted contact" at their bank, so the bank can call one of us if
>    something looks off
>
> None of it takes anything away from them. Can we do it when we're all there for
> the holidays?

---

## To a neighbor or friend

> Did you get one of those texts about an unpaid toll? I've gotten four this
> month. Apparently they're everywhere right now.
>
> Rule I've started using: never tap a link in a message I didn't ask for. If it's
> real I can always find it another way.

---

## Email to a facility or senior center

**Subject: Free scam-awareness materials for residents**

> Hello,
>
> I'm a volunteer who gives a short talk on scams targeting older adults. I put
> together some materials that are free for anyone to use — a one-page sheet in
> large type designed to go on a refrigerator, wallet cards, and a 5-minute
> script that any staff member can read at a residents' meeting without any
> special training.
>
> Everything is free and there is nothing to buy. You're welcome to put your own
> name and front desk number on it. Available in English, Spanish, Vietnamese,
> Russian, and Chinese.
>
> Happy to come give the longer talk in person if that would be useful, or just
> send you the files to use however you like.
>
> [Your name]

## Bulletin or newsletter blurb (100 words)

> **Three steps that stop almost every scam**
>
> Americans over 60 reported losing $7.7 billion to scams last year. The people
> running these are professionals — so the answer isn't to outsmart them. It's to
> be slower than them.
>
> Before any money moves: **Look up the number yourself** — not the one they gave
> you. **Call the person yourself** — hang up first. **Wait a day** — real
> problems survive a night's sleep.
>
> You don't have to get suspicious of everybody. You just add one step.
>
> If something has already happened: **833-372-8311**, free, no judgment.

---

## The one-line version

For the bottom of an email, a fridge magnet, a church bulletin footer:

> **Hang up. Look up the number yourself. Wait a day.**

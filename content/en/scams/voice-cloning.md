# A voice that sounds exactly like family

## What changed

For most of the history of phone scams, the defense was "that doesn't sound like
him."

That defense is gone.

Software that copies a person's voice is now free, takes about a minute to use,
and needs only a few seconds of recorded audio. That audio comes from anywhere —
a Facebook video, a TikTok, a voicemail greeting, a church livestream, a wedding
video, a work presentation on YouTube.

It copies the accent, the rhythm, the little verbal habits. On a phone speaker,
during a crying call, at 11 at night, it is not distinguishable from the real
person. Trained listeners can't do it reliably. You will not be able to do it.

The FBI logged over 3,100 complaints from people over 60 in 2025 mentioning AI,
with more than $352 million in losses — and that only counts cases where the
victim later realized AI was involved. Most never find out.

---

## Where you'll meet it

**The emergency call.** A grandchild, a son, a niece — crying, in trouble, needing
money. See [grandparent scam](/scams/grandparent-scam).

**The boss or the pastor.** A voice you know asking you to handle something
urgent and quiet.

**The video call.** Real-time face-swapping exists now too. A short, glitchy video
call is no longer proof.

**The celebrity endorsement.** A famous investor's voice recommending a platform
in a social media ad. He never said it. See
[investment scams](/scams/investment-and-crypto).

---

## The defense, and why it holds

You cannot beat this by listening harder. So don't try. Beat it with something
the software cannot have: **shared history that was never recorded.**

### The code word

One word or phrase. Told out loud, in person, to your family. Never texted, never
posted, never emailed.

When a voice calls in distress, you ask for it.

An AI can copy your grandson's voice perfectly. It cannot know what your family
calls the back bedroom, or the name of the lake you went to in 1998, because
those things were never said out loud on the internet.

**Set it at a meal this week. Four minutes. It defeats the entire category.**

### The callback

Even better, and needs no preparation: **hang up and call them back** on the
number you've always used.

Voice cloning works on the call they place. It cannot answer the call you place.
That single sentence is the whole defense, and it's the same Step 2 that protects
you from everything else on this site.

### The question with a wrong answer in it

If you have neither, ask something with a false premise:

> "Is this about the car you borrowed from me last month?"

There was no car. A real grandson says "what car?" A scammer, working from a
script and eager to agree, says "yes, exactly."

---

## What not to bother with

**Don't try to hear the fake.** You won't. Listening harder is not a plan, and
believing you can tell is worse than admitting you can't — it makes you confident
at the wrong moment.

**Don't rely on a video call.** Ask them to turn their head sideways or wave a
hand in front of their face if you like — today's fakes still struggle with it —
but assume that gap closes soon.

**Don't panic about your own voice being out there.** You can't recall it and it
doesn't matter. The defense was never keeping your voice private. It's the
callback and the code word.

---

## For families

Three things, once, and you're done:

1. **A code word** everyone knows.
2. **A standing rule:** any emergency money request gets a hang-up and a callback.
   No exceptions, including from you.
3. **Set social media to friends-only**, especially accounts belonging to
   grandchildren, whose voices are the ones most often cloned.

## Technology moved. You still win.

The voice can be copied. The face can be copied. But a callback to a number you
already had, and a word your family said out loud at a dinner table — those
cannot be copied by anything that exists or is coming.

You don't need to keep up with the technology. You need a code word and a habit
of calling back, and both of those will still work in twenty years.

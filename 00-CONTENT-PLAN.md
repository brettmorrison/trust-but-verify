## 1. The single idea the whole site is built on

You do not have to become a suspicious person. You add **one step** before money
or access moves.

Everything on this site — every page, handout, card, and script — is a different
doorway into the same three steps:

1. **Look up the number yourself.**
2. **Call the person yourself.**
3. **Wait a day.**

And the same three signals that tell you to use them:

- **It came to you.** You did not start this.
- **It moved you fast.** Fear, urgency, love, or excitement, arriving quickly.
- **It wants a transfer.** Money, a code, a password, or access to your computer.

Three steps, three signals. Nothing else on the site is allowed to compete with
these for attention.

---

## 2. Why the site is organized the way it is

The brand name's job is memorability *after* first contact — after the talk,
after the handout, after a neighbor says "there's a website for this."

Search traffic is a separate job, and it is won by **question pages**: pages
titled the way a worried person actually types. Nobody searches "trust but
verify." They search *"is the text from Chase about a $500 charge real."*

So the site has two front doors:

| Door | Who arrives | What they need |
|---|---|---|
| **Brand door** (home, the three steps, printables) | Someone who saw the talk or got a handout | A method they can remember and hand to a friend |
| **Question door** (question pages) | Someone mid-panic at 9pm with a text on their screen | A yes-or-no answer in the first sentence |

Question pages must answer in the **first line**, before any explanation.
Someone frightened will not scroll.

## 4. Language plan

Chosen for the Houston metro and for the people most likely to be handed a
printout at a facility desk. Spanish first and by a wide margin.

13 languages are now drafted. Full status, per-language notes, and the
maintenance plan live in **TRANSLATIONS.md** — that file is the source of truth
for what is validated and what is not.

| Tier | Languages |
|---|---|
| Validated | English |
| Drafted, awaiting a native reader | Spanish, Vietnamese, Chinese (Simplified), Russian, Ukrainian, French, German, Portuguese, Polish, Romanian, Arabic, Indonesian |
| Wanted next, draft only when a validator is waiting | Korean, Tagalog, Urdu, Hindi, Farsi, Haitian Creole, Amharic, Somali, Traditional Chinese |

Each drafted language has the core page, a fridge sheet, and a wallet card.

Translation rules used throughout:

- These are **rewrites, not translations**. "Wait a day" carries different weight
  in each language; the sentence that lands is the sentence that ships.
- Never translate a phone number, agency name, or website into the target
  language. A person calling 833-372-8311 needs the digits, not a rendering.
- Where a US agency has no equivalent concept in the reader's home country,
  say what the agency *does* rather than what it is called.
- Keep sentences short enough to be read aloud by a family member who is
  translating on the spot.

### Validation status — the rule

**Every non-English page is marked as an unvalidated AI translation until the
project maintainer confirms a native speaker has validated it.** No exceptions, no soft wording.

Each translated page carries, in its own language, above the content:

- that it was machine-translated
- that no native speaker has checked it
- that it should not be printed or distributed yet
- a request for a native speaker to help, with a contact address

The same notice appears as a bordered band on every non-English fridge sheet and
as a line on every non-English wallet card. The English versions carry no band —
they're validated.

Frontmatter on each translated file carries `status:` and `validated_by:` fields.
When the project maintainer confirms validation, both change and the banner
comes off; if the page is later revised, the banner goes back on.

Recruitment page: `/help-translate`. Contact:
**translations@trustbutverifyproject.org** — see BACKLOG.md for whether
the mailbox itself (Cloudflare Email Routing) is confirmed set up yet.

**Four validated languages beat fourteen unvalidated ones.**

## 6. Editorial rules

1. **Never say "obviously."** Every scam is obvious afterward and invisible during.
2. **No shame, ever.** Not in the copy, not in the examples, not implied. Shame is
   the reason most of this goes unreported, which means shame is a co-conspirator.
3. **Reading level: 6th grade.** Not because readers are simple — because
   frightened people read at a lower level than calm people.
4. **Type size 18pt minimum in print, 20px minimum on screen.** Non-negotiable.
5. **Numbers as digits.** "833-372-8311," never "eight three three."
6. **One action per page.** If a page asks for two things it will get neither.
7. **Every claim sourced.** Federal data only for statistics: FBI IC3, FTC.
   Cite the year. Update every April when the new IC3 report drops.
8. **No fear as a closer.** Fear is the scammer's tool. Pages end on capability.

## 7. What still needs a human

- Native-speaker review of all four translations
- Legal review before using the word "nonprofit" in any public copy
- A decision on whether the site collects any visitor data at all
  (recommendation: it should not — no analytics, no forms, no cookies; the
  privacy posture *is* the credibility)

# What The Beekeeper Gets Right (and Wrong) About the Scam That Starts It

<img class="post-poster" src="/blog-assets/the-beekeeper-poster.jpg" alt="Theatrical poster for The Beekeeper (2024), showing Jason Statham in silhouette surrounded by bees against a yellow background">

*The Beekeeper* (2024), starring Jason Statham, is a revenge action movie —
not a documentary. But the scam that sets the whole plot in motion is
closer to real life than most of what Hollywood does with this subject.
We watched it the way we check everything else on this site: against FBI
and FTC data, not against how dramatic it looks.

**Spoiler note:** this covers the setup, which happens in the first ten
minutes and is in every trailer. It doesn't spoil the ending.

## The setup

Eloise Parker, a retired schoolteacher, is working on her laptop when a
pop-up fills her screen: a virus warning, with a phone number to call. She
calls it. The "technician" walks her through installing remote-access
software, then talks her into typing in banking and password information.
By the time she realizes what's happened, her personal savings and over
$2 million from a charity she manages are gone. Ashamed, she hasn't told
anyone. She takes her own life. Her tenant — a quiet beekeeper who turns
out to be a former government operative — decides to find the people
responsible.

Everything after that is fiction. The first ten minutes are not.

## What it gets right

**The fake virus pop-up.** This is the single most accurate detail in the
movie. A browser pop-up designed to look like a system alert, with a
phone number instead of a real fix, is one of the most common scams
running today — see [the fake security popup](/scams/tech-support-popup)
for exactly how it works and what to do when you see one. The movie
doesn't exaggerate this part at all.

**Remote-access software as the actual weapon.** The "technician" doesn't
need to break into anything. He asks Eloise to install a real, legitimate
remote-desktop program — the same category of tool IT departments use
for real support — and she lets him in herself. That's accurate. Once
someone has remote access, they can see everything on the screen,
including a password being typed. If this happens to you, [these are the
exact steps to take](/questions/i-let-someone-into-my-computer).

**A trained, organized operation — not one bad actor.** The movie shows a
call center: rows of headsets, scripts, quotas, a manager walking the
floor. That part is real. Federal prosecutions describe exactly this kind
of structure, and it's why this site never blames the person who was
scammed — they were up against professionals, not luck.

**The shame and silence.** Eloise doesn't tell anyone before it's too
late. That's the single most realistic and most important thing the movie
gets right. Shame is what keeps this crime working — it's the reason
[we say on the about page](/about) that no one should have to read
anything here without flinching, and it's the whole reason a page like
[I think I was scammed](/i-think-i-was-scammed) starts with "this is not
your fault" before anything else.

## What it exaggerates

**The ending.** A former operative hunting down a call center and its
leadership one by one is an action movie, not a real option, and it
shouldn't be. The real version of "making it right" is boring by
comparison: call your bank immediately and ask them to recall the
payment, report it to the [FBI's Internet Crime Complaint
Center](https://www.ic3.gov) and the [FTC](https://reportfraud.ftc.gov),
and freeze your credit. None of that makes for a good trailer. All of it
is more likely to actually help than anything in the movie.

**The scale, tied to a single conspiracy at the top.** The movie's scam
operation turns out to be connected to a tech billionaire and, eventually,
the president's own family — one organization, one villain, one
takedown. Real elder fraud isn't one conspiracy; it's thousands of
loosely connected operations, many overseas, running the same handful of
scripts on millions of people at once. The FBI's Internet Crime Complaint
Center put reported losses from Americans over 60 at **$7.7 billion in a
single year**. There's no one building to burn down. That's what makes
[the three steps](/#scam-types) — not a hero — the actual defense.

**How fast it all resolves.** In the movie, the money and the truth both
come out within days. In reality, money sent to a scammer is very often
gone for good, and recovery — when it happens — takes months, not a
weekend. This is also why the three steps matter so much: they work
*before* money moves, because almost nothing works well after.

## The one thing worth taking from it

Skip the revenge plot. Keep this: Eloise had no reason to distrust the
person on the phone, right up until it was too late. That's not a
character flaw the movie invented for the plot — it's how this actually
works. **The defense was never supposed to be catching the lie in the
moment.** It's a habit, set in advance: hang up, look up the number
yourself, wait a day. [Here's the whole method.](/#scam-types)

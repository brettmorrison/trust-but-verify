# "There's a problem with your account"

This is the most frequently reported scam by Americans over 60. Not the costliest
— but the one that arrives most often, in the most disguises.

## How it arrives

A text, an email, or a voicemail about a problem:

- A charge you didn't make — reply YES or NO
- Your account is locked or suspended
- Your password is expiring
- A payment failed and your service will be cut off
- Your package couldn't be delivered
- An unpaid toll
- Verify your details to keep your benefits
- Your antivirus subscription auto-renewed for $499 — call to cancel

They impersonate whoever you're most likely to have: Amazon, your bank, Netflix,
USPS, Apple, Medicare, Microsoft, your electric company, PayPal.

## What they want

One of three things, and they're happy with any of them:

1. **Your password** — via a link to a fake login page that looks perfect
2. **Your verification code** — the six digits your bank texts you
3. **A phone call from you** — which turns into one of the bigger schemes on this
   site

That third one is the growing version, and the sneakiest. There's no link to
inspect and no fake website to spot. Just a phone number and a story. Security
advice that's all about "don't click links" doesn't help you here.

## The shapes it takes

Same scam, different vehicles. Each shape has its own way of looking harmless.

### The fake "verify your account" email

The oldest one, and still the most common. A message that looks exactly like one
from a company you use, with a button that says Verify, Confirm, Review, or
Update. The button goes to a copy of the real login page. You type your password
into the copy, and the copy belongs to them.

The copy is often perfect. Logos, fonts, footer text, even the little
unsubscribe link at the bottom are lifted straight from a real email. **A page
looking right is not evidence. Looking right is the entire product.**

### The text message

The same thing, shorter, and harder to examine because a phone screen hides most
of the address. The package, the toll, and the recall are the three you will see
most often, and [those have a page of their own](/scams/delivery-toll-recall-texts).

### The spoofed sender

The name you see is not the address it came from. Mail programs show a friendly
name, and whoever sends the message picks that name themselves. "Chase Fraud
Alert" can sit on top of an address like alerts.chase-security@mail-b7.info.

On a phone this matters more, because the friendly name is usually all you are
shown. Tapping the sender name is what reveals the address underneath, and most
people never tap it.

### The link versus the attachment

A link takes you somewhere. An attachment brings something to you. Both are used,
and the attachment is the one people expect less.

It arrives as an invoice, a receipt, a fax, a voicemail, a shipping label, or a
court notice, in a PDF, a Word document, or a ZIP file. Some attachments simply
hold the same fake link inside, which carries the message past filters that only
check links. Others ask you to enable editing or enable content when you open
them, and that second click is the one that installs something.

**You did not order an invoice you were not expecting.** A document that arrives
out of nowhere gets the same treatment as a link: leave it closed, reach the
company your own way, and ask whether they sent you anything.

### The QR code

Newer, and it works because a square of dots gives you nothing to read. There is
no address to squint at, so there is no wrongness to notice.

They turn up as stickers pasted over the real code on a parking meter or a
charging station, printed on flyers and fake utility shutoff notices, and inside
emails as a picture, which slips past software that only inspects links.

Your phone will usually show you the address before it opens anything. **Read
that address before you tap Open.** If it is not the company you expected, close
it. And a code that arrives in a message you did not ask for is simply a link in
a message you did not ask for. Do not use it at all.

### The "was this you?" alert

The cleverest of the set, because it borrows the shape of the message that is
supposed to protect you.

> "Security alert: your account was accessed from a new device in Dallas, TX. If
> this was not you, secure your account here."

Nothing has happened. There is no sign-in. The alarm is the bait, and "No, this
was not me" is the button they are hoping for, because it leads to a login page
that is theirs.

The same trick arrives as a password reset you never requested, and as a
verification prompt on your phone that keeps buzzing until you approve it just to
make it stop.

**Never approve a prompt you did not start.** A reset you did not ask for is not
a task, it is a notice. Ignore the message, open the account the way you always
open it, and look for yourself.

## The tells

Every one of these is a reason to slow down and look. **None of them is proof, in
either direction.** A message carrying three of these signs can still be your own
bank having a bad morning, and a message carrying none of them can still be a
scam. A tell is a signal to go check for yourself. It is never a verdict, and it
is never a reason to feel foolish for having believed something well made.

**The link is wrong, and the wrongness is small.** Real: chase.com. Fake:
chase-secure-alerts.com, chase.verify-account.net, chasebank-support.co.
Everything before the last dot before the slash can be faked; only the part
immediately before the first single slash is the real owner.

Better rule: **don't inspect links at all.** Don't get good at spotting fakes.
Just never use one. Type the address yourself, or use your own bookmark, or your
bank's app.

**The greeting is generic.** "Dear Customer," "Dear Account Holder." Your bank
knows your name.

**Urgency plus a deadline.** 24 hours. Immediately. Or your account closes.

**Small errors.** Odd grammar, a strange space before punctuation, a logo slightly
the wrong color. Note: the good ones have none of these anymore. AI writes clean
English now. **Absence of errors proves nothing.**

**The only way forward is inside the message.** Every route it offers you, the
button and the number and the code and the attachment, lives in the message
itself. Real companies are glad for you to reach them your own way. A message
that needs you to stay inside it is telling you something.

**It arrived out of nowhere.** You weren't expecting a package. You don't drive
that toll road. You don't have that subscription. Though sometimes you do — they
send thousands and some land right.

## How to look without touching anything

You do not need this part. The rule below handles every case with no inspection
at all. But people like to look, and looking is safe as long as you never tap.

**On a computer, hover.** Rest the mouse pointer on the link without clicking.
The real address appears in the bottom corner of the window, or in a small box
beside the pointer.

**On a phone, press and hold.** Hold your finger on the link for a second instead
of tapping it. A panel opens showing the full address, with a Copy option and a
Cancel. Read it, then Cancel.

**To see the real sender, tap or click the sender name.** The friendly name opens
up to show the actual address underneath.

**Read an address from the right, not the left.** Find the first single slash.
The two words immediately before it are the owner of the page, and nothing else
is:

- chase.com/login is Chase
- secure.chase.com/alerts is Chase
- chase.secure-login.net/verify is secure-login.net, not Chase
- chase-com.info/account is chase-com.info, not Chase

Everything to the left of those two words can say absolutely anything. That is
the whole trick, and it is why the fakes read so well.

Now put all of that away. **Knowing how to inspect a link is a party trick.
Never using one is a defense.** The people who get caught are not the people who
could not have told. They are the people who were tired, or busy, or in a hurry,
and gave it one second instead of five.

## What to do

**Never use the link. Never use the number in the message.**

Instead:

- Open your bank's app, or type the address you already know
- Call the number on the back of your card
- Log in the way you always log in

If the alert is real, it will be waiting for you inside your account. If there's
nothing there, there was nothing there.

**Never read a verification code to anyone.** Your bank sends those to keep other
people out. Nobody legitimate will ever ask you to say one out loud or type it
into a chat. Anyone asking is standing at your bank's front door trying to get
you to hand them the key.

**Don't reply STOP to a scam text.** It confirms a live person reads that number.
Delete it, or report it by forwarding to 7726 (SPAM) — free on all US carriers.

## The three steps, applied to a message

This site has [one method](/the-three-steps), and it works here exactly the way
it works on a phone call.

**1. Look it up yourself.**
Not the link. Not the number in the message. Not the address in the signature.
Open the app you already have, or type in the address you already know, or use
the number on the back of your card.

**2. Reach the company yourself.**
Close the message first, then get in touch your own way, and ask plainly: *"I
got a message saying there is a problem with my account. Did that come from
you?"* If the alert is real, it will be waiting for you inside your account. If
nothing is there, there was nothing there.

**3. Wait a day.**
Almost nothing inside a real account needs an answer tonight. A real fraud hold,
a real overdue bill, and a real recall all survive a night's sleep. The deadline
in the message is the one part of it that is reliably fake.

The message wants all three steps to happen inside the message: its link, its
number, its clock. **Taking any single one of them outside is enough to end it.**

## If you clicked and entered your password

1. Change that password immediately — on the real site, typed yourself
2. If you use that password anywhere else, change it there too. This is the real
   damage. One password, reused, unlocks a whole life.
3. Change your **email** password first if it was the same one
4. Turn on two-factor authentication where offered
5. Watch statements for three months

You are not in trouble. You typed something into a box. Millions of people do it
every year, including people whose job is computer security.


## If you opened an attachment

Opening the file is usually not the moment of damage. The damage comes from what
it asked you to do next.

1. If you opened it and closed it and nothing else happened, you are most likely
   fine. Delete it.
2. If you enabled editing, enabled content, allowed a download, or a program
   installed itself, run a scan with the security software already on your
   computer and have someone you trust take a look.
3. If you typed a password in order to view the document, treat it exactly like
   the section above. Change that password, and change it everywhere you reused
   it.

## The one change that makes this stop mattering

Reusing the same password everywhere is why one stolen password becomes ten
stolen accounts. A password manager fixes that permanently: it invents a
different, harder password for every site and remembers all of them, so you
only have to remember one.

You likely already have one, free, built into a device you own — Apple
("Passwords" on iPhone and Mac), Google ("Password Manager" in Chrome and
Android), and Microsoft (in Windows and Edge) all include one. You don't have
to buy or install anything. Ask someone you trust to help you turn it on and
move your most important passwords — email and banking first — into it.

## The habit that ends this whole category

Never use a link or number that came to you. Reach the company your own way,
every time, without exception.

That's not vigilance and it doesn't require you to inspect anything. It's one
habit, and once you have it, this entire category of scam simply stops working on
you — today's version and every version they invent next.
